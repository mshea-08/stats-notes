8  Sampling

8.1 Population Versus Sample

A population is the entire group of individuals/items we wish to investigate and draw conclusions about. It is often unreasonable or impossible to observe or measure all individuals of a population, so in practice we take a sample of individuals. In Topic 3, we addressed the importance of random sampling as a way to help draw fair conclusions.

Example 8.1 A city wants to determine the need for additional bus routes among city residents. Since it isn’t practical to ask everyone, the transportation department randomly selects 800 households and asks how often they would use a new bus route if one were added near them.

  • Population: all residents of the city.
  • Sample: the 800 randomly selected households that were surveyed.

Example 8.2 A quality control engineer at a factory wants to know the average lifetime of the light bulbs produced on a particular assembly line. Testing a bulb means running it until it burns out, so testing every bulb would leave nothing to sell. Instead, the engineer pulls 50 bulbs at random from the week’s production and records how many hours each one lasts.

  • Population: all light bulbs produced on that assembly line.
  • Sample: the 50 bulbs that were tested.

Example 8.3 A university wants to know how many hours per week its undergraduates spend working at paid jobs. The registrar’s office randomly selects 300 students from the enrollment list and sends them a short survey.

  • Population: all undergraduate students enrolled at the university.
  • Sample: the 300 students selected to receive the survey.

The goal of taking a sample is to try to draw conclusions about the entire population, however we should not expect the sample to exactly reveal the underlying population behavior. Moreover, from sample to sample, we expect variation. This is known as sampling variability.

Example 8.4 Four research nurses each record the systolic blood pressure of 100 randomly selected adults visiting the same hospital’s outpatient clinic.

We see that each of the nurses records a slightly different mean and distribution. The population in this case is all the clinic’s patients, however each nurse only sees a sample of that. This is sample variability.

A parameter is a number that describes the population, while a statistic is a number that describes a sample. We may not know parameters, but they have some underlying single value. A statistic, depends on the sample I take. In the above example, each nurse records a different mean. These means are statistics and they depend on the sample that was taken.

Parameter (Population) Statistic (Sample)
Proportion \(p\) \(\hat{p}\)
Mean \(\mu\) \(\bar{x}\)
Standard Deviation \(\sigma\) \(s\)

A principle question we want to answer is how close are the statistics to the parameters? For example, how close are the nurse’s means to the actual population mean (a parameter).

ImportantCategorical Versus Quantitative

A proportion is the statistic of interest when our data is categorical. For example, we might want to know what proportion of the population is left-handed, allergic to peanuts, or has type A blood.

Mean and standard deviation are relevant when the data is quantitative.

8.2 Sample Proportion

We will start off by talking about sample proportions and categorical data. Consider the following examples…

Example 8.5 A college wants to know what proportion of their most recent graduates have a job six months after graduating.† The college randomly samples 500 graduates and finds that 64% of graduates in the sample have a job, so \(\hat{p} = 0.64\).

†This is categorical because we just want to know if they have a job after 6 months (yes/no).

We can think of the sample proportion as a random variable that changes depending on the sample I take.

Example 8.6 Claudia and Joey want to know what proportion of their classmates have already finished Homework 4. Claudia samples 20 peers and finds that 32% of them have finished Homework 4. Joey samples a different 20 peers and finds that 39% of them have finished Homework 4.

If we collected a bunch of sample proportions from different random samples, the distribution of these values would be approximately normal. This is a result of the Central Limit Theorem. The mean of all the sample proportions \(\hat{p}\) is the population proportion \(p\) and the standard deviation is,

\[\sigma_{\hat{p}} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}\]

where \(n\) is the size of the sample we take.

Example 8.7 The proportion of left-handed people in the population is approximately 0.1. Suppose we wish to take a sample of 100 people, what would be the mean and standard deviation of the sample proportion?

What if we took a sample of 200 people?

TipAnswer

The mean is 0.1, since this is the population proportion. If we too a sample of 100 people the sample standard deviation would be

\[\sigma_{\hat{p}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.1(0.9)}{100}} = 0.03.\]

If we took a sample of 200 people, the sample standard deviation would shrink, \[\sigma_{\hat{p}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.1(0.9)}{200}} = 0.0212.\]

The more people we sample, the less likely our sample proportion will be far from the population proportion!

Example 8.8 If I sample 100 people about their handedness, what is the probability that more than 15% of them will be left-handed?

TipAnswer

The sample proportion is approximately normal with mean 0.1 and standard deviation 0.055. We can use the normal calculator on StatCrunch to compute \(P(\hat{p} > 0.15)\). We get,

\[P(\hat{p} > 0.15) = 0.0478.\]

Example 8.9 If I sample 200 people about their handedness, what is the probability that more than 15% of them will be left-handed?

TipAnswer

The sample proportion is approximately normal with mean 0.1 and standard deviation 0.0212. We can use the normal calculator on StatCrunch to compute \(P(\hat{p} > 0.15)\). We get,

\[P(\hat{p} > 0.15) = 0.0092\]

We said that the sample proportion is approximately normal and we should be cautious under what conditions it is appropriate to use this approximation. If our sample is genuinely random, we can apply this normal approximation if \(np \geq 10\) and \(n(1-p) \geq 10\).

Example 8.10 According to the CDC National Diabetes Statistic Report, 11.6% of the U.S. population has diabetes. If I sample 125 people, how likely is my sample proportion to be between 10.6% and 12.6%?

TipAnswer

First we must compute the standard deviation,

\[\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.116(0.884)}{125}} = 0.0286.\]

Now we want to compute, \(P(0.106 < \hat{p} < 0.126) = 0.27339888\).

Example 8.11 Using the same set up as Example 8.11, find \(c\) such that \(P(0.116-c < \hat{p} < 0.116+c) = 0.95\).

I’m looking for an interval that is centered around the mean (0.116), such that the is a 95% chance of my sample proportion being within this interval.

TipAnswer

We can use the Empirical rule: 95% of the data should fall within two standard deviations of the mean. That means,

\[c = 2 \times 0.0286 = 0.0572\].

In other words, there is a 95% chance that the sample proportion will fall between \(0.116-0.0572 = 0.0588\) and \(0.116+0.0572 = 0.1732\).

We can also use the StatCrunch normal calculator above. On the Between setting set the probability to 0.95–this will always give an interval that is centered around the mean.

ImportantWhat if the population proportion is unknown?

A more relevant question to researchers is: Given a sample proportion \(\hat{p}\), how close should we expect the population proportion to be?

This is something we cannot answer yet! But we will address in Topic 6.
