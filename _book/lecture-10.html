10  Drawing Conclusions from Sample Data

The goal of this topic is to draw conclusions about the population parameters when we only have information about the sample statistics. This is of course a practical problem!

Today we will assume that the data we are dealing with is quantitative, therefore the statistic of interest will be the sample mean \(\bar{x}\).

10.1 Point Estimation

The easiest, but least justified, way to relate the sample to the population is through point estimation. In point estimation, we estimate that the population mean is the same as the sample mean.

The trustworthiness of our point estimation relies on the quality of our sampling and sample size. In general, we should be more confident that the population mean is close to the sample mean if:

  • We have taken a random sample that accurately represents all individuals in the population.
  • We have taken a large random sample. The larger the better.

Example 10.1 A high school with 1,200 students wants to estimate the average number of hours of sleep students get on a school night. Four different groups collected data and calculated a sample mean. Which point estimate should the school be most confident in?

  1. 15 students were randomly selected from the school roster and surveyed. The sample mean was 7.1 hours.
  2. An online poll was posted on the school website, and 350 students chose to respond. The sample mean was 6.5 hours.
  3. 120 students were randomly selected from the school roster and surveyed. The sample mean was 6.9 hours.
  4. All 30 students in one AP Calculus class were surveyed. The sample mean was 6.2 hours.

Example 10.2 A snack company fills bags of chips that are supposed to weigh 10 ounces. A quality control team wants to estimate the mean weight of all bags produced during one week. Which point estimate should the team be most confident in?

  1. An inspector weighed the first 400 bags that came off the line Monday morning. The sample mean was 10.3 ounces.
  2. 150 bags were randomly selected from all shifts throughout the week and weighed. The sample mean was 9.9 ounces.
  3. A worker picked 40 bags that “looked full” and weighed them. The sample mean was 10.4 ounces.
  4. 12 bags were randomly selected from all shifts throughout the week and weighed. The sample mean was 9.7 ounces.

10.2 Confidence Intervals

In Topic 5 we were able to make statements like the following.

Example 10.4 Suppose the average commute time for residents of a city is 32 minutes with a standard deviation of 9 minutes. If I sample 36 residents randomly then there is a 90% chance the sample mean will be between __ and __.

TipAnswer

The sample mean is normally distributed with mean 32 (same as population) and standard deviation,

\[ \frac{9}{\sqrt{36}} = \frac{9}{6} = 1.5 \]

We can use the Normal Calculator to compute the bounds, or we can recognize that for the standard normal distribution, 90% is between the z-scores \(z = -1.645\) and \(z = 1.645\). So the bounds are,

\[32 - 1.645 (1.5) = 29.5325\]

and

\[32 + 1.645 (1.5) = 34.4675.\]

So, there is a 90% chance the sample mean will be between 29.53 and 34.47.

It will be more interesting, however to answer the reverse problem: Given a sample mean, can we give an interval expressing where we expect the population mean to fall? In order to do this we need to pick a confidence level. Common confidence levels are 90% and 95%.

To compute a confidence interval we need the following conditions to hold:

  • The sample is random.
  • The sample size is large or the variable of interest is normally distributed.

How we compute the confidence interval depends on what information we have access to. To start, assume we know the sample mean, \(\bar{x}\), and the population standard deviation, \(\sigma\). Then given a confidence level, we can compute the confidence interval as,

\[(\bar{x} - z_{\text{CL}}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}, \bar{x} + z_{\text{CL}}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}})\]

where \(z_{\text{CL}}\) is the appropriate \(z\)-score for whatever confidence level we choose.

Confidence Level \(z_{\text{CL}}\)
90% 1.645
95% 1.96

Example 10.5 Suppose average pizza delivery times are normally distributed with an unknown population mean and a population standard deviation of six minutes. A random sample of 28 pizza delivery restaurants is taken and has a sample mean delivery time of 36 minutes. Compute a 90% confidence interval for the population mean.

TipAnswer

Our sample is small, but since we know that the population is normally distributed we are okay to proceed.

We have the population standard deviation, so we can use the above formula

\[36 - 1.645 \times \frac{6}{\sqrt{28}} = 34.135, \; 36 + 1.645 \times \frac{6}{\sqrt{28}} = 37.865.\]

We estimate with 90% confidence that the population mean is between 34.135 and 37.865 minutes.

Example 10.6 A coffee shop chain wants to estimate the average time customers spend waiting for their order. Assume the population standard deviation of wait times is \(\sigma = 14\) minutes. A manager takes a random sample of 49 customers and finds a sample mean wait time \(\bar{x} = 128.5\) seconds. Construct a 95% confidence interval for the true mean wait time.

TipAnswer

We have the population standard deviation and this time \(z_{\text{CL}} = 1.96\),

\[128.5 - 1.96 \times \frac{14}{\sqrt{49}} = 124.58, \; 128.5 + 1.96 \times \frac{14}{\sqrt{49}} = 132.42.\]

We estimate with 95% confidence that the true mean wait time is between 124.58 and 132.42 minutes.

It’s a bit strange to assume we know the population standard deviation, so suppose we are interested in the same question but only have the the sample mean, \(\bar{x}\), and sample standard deviation, \(s\).

In the case where we knew the population standard deviation, the \(z\)-score is given by

\[z = \frac{\bar{x}-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}.\]

If we don’t know the population standard deviation, the best we can do is replace it with the sample standard deviation

\[t = \frac{\bar{x}-\mu}{s/\sqrt{n}}.\]

But \(s\), like \(\bar{x}\), is random, so the value above varies more than a \(z\)-score would. It turns out we should consider \(t\) as coming from the student-t distribution (instead of \(z\), which comes from the normal distribution).

So in order to compute the confidence interval when we only have the sample mean and standard deviation, we must use \(t\)-values instead od \(z\)-values. The confidence interval is then,

\[(\bar{x} - t_{\text{CL},n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}, \bar{x} + t_{\text{CL},n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}})\]

The \(t\)-score, \(t_{\text{CL},n-1}\), depends on the confidence level AND the degrees of freedom which is \(n-1\). Below we list the \(t\)-scores for various values of \(n-1\) and the confidence levels 90% and 90%,

Degrees of Freedom, \(n-1\) 90% Confidence Level, \(t_{90,n-1}\) 95% Confidence Level, \(t_{95,n-1}\)
5 2.01505 2.57058
10 1.81246 2.22814
15 1.75305 2.13145
20 1.72472 2.08596
30 1.69726 2.04227
40 1.68385 2.02108

Since the \(t\)-score depends both on the confidence level and the degrees of freedom, we need a computer/calculator to compute it.

Example 10.7 A random sample of 32 college students reported how many minutes they spend on their phones before bed. The sample mean was \(\bar{x}=72\) minutes and the sample standard deviation was \(s=10\) minutes. Construct a 95% confidence interval for the true mean.

TipAnswer

Our sample is large (\(n = 32 \geq 30\)), so by the Central Limit Theorem we are okay to proceed even without knowing the population is normally distributed.

We do not have the population standard deviation, only the sample standard deviation, so we use the \(t\) distribution with \(n - 1 = 31\) degrees of freedom. For 95% confidence, \(t_{\text{CL}} = 2.040\).

\[72 - 2.040 \times \frac{10}{\sqrt{32}} = 68.39, \; 72 + 2.040 \times \frac{10}{\sqrt{32}} = 75.61.\]

We estimate with 95% confidence that the population mean is between 68.39 and 75.61 minutes.
